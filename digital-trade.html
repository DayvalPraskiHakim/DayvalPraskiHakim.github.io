PPMLHDFE Bilateral Trade Data Governance

Digital Trade and Geopolitical Regime Divergence

A gravity-model test of whether digital regulatory divergence — specifically, government-imposed data localization — acts as a structural barrier to bilateral trade, independent of the usual gravity factors like distance, size, and shared borders.

The question

Gravity models explain most of bilateral trade with distance, GDP, and shared history. This project asks what's left over once those are controlled for — testing several distinct channels of digital and geopolitical friction (data-privacy divergence, divergence in market governance model along an open/liberal-vs-state-directed spectrum anchored by the US and China as reference poles, and hardware/export-control barriers) to see which, if any, independently suppress trade once the standard gravity factors are absorbed.

Methodology

Estimated via Pseudo-Poisson Maximum Likelihood with high-dimensional fixed effects (PPMLHDFE) — the standard for gravity estimation with zero trade flows and heteroskedastic errors. Exporter-year and importer-year fixed effects absorb multilateral resistance terms in the baseline specification (Model 1); a second specification (Model 2) adds pair fixed effects to absorb any time-invariant bilateral factors, isolating each friction variable's effect from unobserved, fixed country-pair characteristics. A third specification (Model 2b) restricts to only the friction variables with enough within-pair variation to be genuinely identified under pair fixed effects, rather than trusting the pair-FE estimate on variables that barely move within a pair.

Finding

The headline number (β = −0.34, p = 0.016) comes from market-regime divergence — how far a country's economic governance sits along a spectrum anchored by an open, market-liberal model at one end (associated with the US) and a more state-directed, closed model at the other (associated with China) — not narrowly from data localization. As constructed, this variable takes the higher of the exporter's and importer's own individual friction scores, so it's best read as "at least one side of this pair has a strict, state-directed regime," not a precise pairwise distance metric. The variable that most directly captures data-privacy/localization policy (privacy_max_friction) is insignificant in the baseline specification (β = 0.099, p = 0.599) and is mechanically excluded from the pair fixed-effects model — it's time-invariant within almost every pair in this sample, so it's perfectly collinear with the pair fixed effects and can't be estimated there at all.

Market-regime divergence's own significance rests on limited identifying variation — only about 11% of pairs switch on this variable across the sample, so the pair-FE estimate leans on a fairly thin slice of "movers." The strictest test (Model 2b), which keeps only the two variables with genuinely sufficient within-pair movement to trust (trade agreements and hardware/export-control friction), finds no significant effect — hardware friction lands at p = 0.453. So the accurate summary is: one friction channel (market-regime divergence) shows a significant, negative effect on trade in the pair-FE specification, but it doesn't survive the strictest identification test, and the more literally "digital trade governance" channel (privacy/localization) was never identified as significant at all.
