Continuous-Time FX Volatility Modeling

An extended Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation for ASEAN-5 exchange rates, with the US 10-year yield built in as an exogenous drift component — ranking each currency by how directly it responds to US monetary shocks.

The question

When US yields move, ASEAN currencies don't all react the same way or at the same speed. This project builds a continuous-time model that separates each currency's baseline mean-reverting dynamics from its specific sensitivity to US monetary policy, then ranks the five currencies on that sensitivity.

Methodology

The standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — mean-reverting, continuous-time — is extended with the US 10-year Treasury yield entering the drift term as an exogenous driver. Parameters are estimated via bounded maximum likelihood across all five ASEAN-5 currencies (IDR, THB, SGD, MYR, PHP), with the bounds enforcing economically sensible mean-reversion speeds and volatility levels rather than letting the optimizer wander into degenerate regions.

5,000 simulated paths of the fitted OU-SDE for USD/IDR over a 60-day horizon. The dashed line marks the current spot rate; the fan shows how quickly uncertainty compounds once the US yield shock enters the drift term.

Fig. 02 Stress Surface — IDR Response

The fitted USD/IDR rate as a joint function of shock velocity and duration, showing how the currency's response compounds with both the size and the speed of the underlying US yield move.

Fig. 03 Capital Flight — 10bps Fed Shock

Expected cumulative depreciation for each ASEAN-5 currency following a 10bps shock to the US 10-year yield, using each currency's fitted β. IDR's slope compounds fastest — roughly 1.5× steeper than THB and 2.5× steeper than MYR, SGD, and PHP by day 30.

Findings

The Indonesian rupiah shows the highest exposure to US monetary shocks of the five currencies (β = 0.019, σ = 0.047) — more than four times as volatile as the Singapore dollar (σ = 0.003), which sits at the other end of the ranking. That spread has direct implications for regional carry trade positioning and capital flow risk: IDR-denominated exposure carries meaningfully more US-driven risk than SGD-denominated exposure, even within the same regional bloc.
