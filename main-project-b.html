Adjusted Net Savings and the Hartwick Rule: Evidence from Indonesia

A test of weak sustainability for Indonesia through adjusted net savings (ANS) — the genuine-savings measure that nets out natural resource depletion and environmental damage from conventional national saving. If ANS stays persistently negative, an economy is, in the Hartwick Rule sense, consuming its productive base rather than maintaining it.

The question

The Hartwick Rule gives a simple sustainability test: an economy can consume exhaustible resources indefinitely only if it reinvests the resource rents into other forms of capital. Adjusted net savings operationalizes that test empirically. This paper asks whether Indonesia's genuine savings trajectory is consistent with weak sustainability, and what's driving deviations from it — commodity cycles, policy shifts, or structural change in the capital base.

Open methodological decision

The paper is at a fork between two credible approaches, and the choice determines everything downstream — data structure, identification strategy, and how structural breaks get handled:

  • Single-country route — an ARDL bounds-testing setup, with a Bai-Perron test for structural breaks and a wavelet decomposition to separate short- and long-run sustainability dynamics within Indonesia alone
  • Panel route — a Pooled Mean Group / Mean Group (PMG/MG) estimator, situating Indonesia against a comparable panel of resource-dependent economies to gain cross-sectional power

The single-country route keeps the analysis tightly institutional and policy-specific; the panel route trades some of that specificity for statistical power and a comparative benchmark. That trade-off is the current decision point.

Why it matters

Indonesia's growth model leans heavily on resource extraction — coal, palm oil, nickel — sectors that show up as income today but as depletion on a genuine-savings ledger. A weak-sustainability reading has direct implications for how resource rents should be reinvested, and for whether current fiscal policy around the extractive sector is compatible with maintaining, rather than drawing down, the country's productive base.
